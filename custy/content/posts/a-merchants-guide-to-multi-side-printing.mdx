Front-only printing is the default a lot of stores start with, and it's the first one worth outgrowing. A shirt isn't one printable surface — it's several — and every side a shopper can't customize is a side that never enters the price at all. Left as the default, that's not caution, it's revenue a store never had the chance to earn.

### Every printable side

Custy supports a full set of print locations per product: front, back, left sleeve, right sleeve, and neck tag. Each side gets its own print area and its own mockup, configured per product in the [Design Lab](/design-lab), so a shopper designing a back print sees an accurate back-of-garment preview rather than a stretched guess based on the front. That accuracy matters as much as the extra revenue — a shopper who can see exactly what a sleeve mark or a neck tag will look like is a shopper who trusts what they're about to buy, and trust is most of what closes a personalized order.

A merchant doesn't have to enable every side on every product. A plain crewneck might only ever need a front and a back; a team jersey might make real use of all five. The point isn't to maximize sides for their own sake — it's to match the sides you turn on to what shoppers on that specific product actually want to personalize, and price and mock up only those.

### Sides are priced — and gated — by plan

Every side a merchant enables carries its own price, configured the same way front prints are: as part of the dynamic pricing rules on that product. How many sides a store can turn on depends on its plan — the Free plan supports a single print side, Starter supports up to two, Growth supports up to six, and Pro removes the cap entirely. Full plan details live on [pricing](/pricing). That structure gives a store room to grow into multi-side selling deliberately: start with a front print, prove out demand, add a back print, and keep expanding sides as the plan allows rather than needing every side priced and ready on day one.

### Designing for each side

Not every side should carry the same kind of design. A front print is the one shoppers and everyone around them see first, so it earns the boldest, largest artwork — a full chest graphic, a big logo, a name across the chest. A back print has more room and less immediate scrutiny, which makes it the natural place for detail: taglines, dates, a full roster of names, anything that rewards a second look. Sleeves and the neck tag are small, glanced-at real estate — a monogram, a small mark, or a single line of text reads clean there, while a busy design just looks cramped in the space. Matching design advice to each side's real size and attention isn't a rule Custy enforces on shoppers; it's the difference between a multi-side product that looks considered and one that looks like the same design pasted across four locations.

### Production stays clean

None of this adds chaos on the production side. Each side a shopper designs exports as its own print-ready file, so a five-color front print and a one-color neck tag arrive as two separate, correctly sized files rather than one file a production team has to cut apart by hand. Whoever runs the press — an in-house team or an outside print partner — gets exactly what they need for each location, per order, without extra prep work standing between a finished design and a finished shirt. That per-side file separation is what makes scaling to five sides no harder to fulfill than scaling to two — the work per order grows with the sides a shopper actually used, not with how many locations the product theoretically supports.

Multi-side printing is one of the more direct paths a merchant with an already-customizable product has toward more revenue per order: no new products to source, no new merchandising to plan, just more of the shirt shoppers already want to make their own. Merchants building out team and event orders in particular — see [who sells with Custy](/use-cases) — tend to lean on multi-side setups the hardest, since a name on the back and a number on the sleeve is often the entire point of the order. Turning on a second or third side, pricing it, and letting shoppers design it is a small setup step that opens up demand a front-only product never gets the chance to capture.
