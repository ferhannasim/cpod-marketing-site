When a shopper builds their own product, they stop comparing it to a shelf of interchangeable items — they're comparing it to nothing, because nobody else has one exactly like it. That shift changes what they're willing to pay. A plain t-shirt is a commodity; a t-shirt with a shopper's own name, artwork, and color choices is something they helped make. For merchants running personalized or print-on-demand stores, that difference shows up directly in average order value — not because personalization is a gimmick, but because it turns every design decision into a small, deliberate upsell.

### Design choices become revenue, automatically

Custy's dynamic pricing is what makes that upsell work without a merchant manually re-pricing every variant. Instead of one flat price per product, pricing responds to what the shopper actually selects in the [Design Lab](/design-lab): which print areas they use, which print method the design requires, and which product options they add. Enable a second print side and the order picks up that side's own price; add a third or fourth, up to whatever the merchant's plan allows, and each one is priced on its own. Choose a different print method for a more detailed design, or add a product option like an upgraded size or material, and the price adjusts to match. None of this is guesswork for the merchant — every rule is configured once, and enforced automatically the moment a shopper starts designing. Plan details for how much of this a store can turn on live on [pricing](/pricing).

### Group orders raise the ceiling

Personalization also reaches order sizes flat pricing never sees. Teams ordering matching jerseys, a wedding party ordering matching shirts, a company ordering hoodies for an event — these are exactly the orders a single, generic product page struggles to convert, because the buyer needs to know the price at ten or fifty units, not one. Custy's quantity discounts let a merchant define percent or fixed discounts across quantity ranges they set themselves, so a group buyer sees the right price the moment they commit to a size range. This isn't just a discount — it's the mechanism that turns a browser who might have bought one item into an organizer who buys many of them at once, which is exactly the kind of order [team and event merch stores](/use-cases) are built around.

### Engagement compounds

There's a second effect worth planning for: shoppers who spend time in a design tool are shoppers who are already invested before checkout. They've made choices, seen a live preview, and effectively pre-approved the product they're about to buy — which lowers hesitation at checkout and lowers the return rate, since the finished item matches exactly what they saw. Merchants who watch this closely tend to see the same shoppers come back to design something new for the next occasion, whether that's a different print area, a new print method, or an upgraded product option. Personalization isn't a one-time bump to average order value; it's a habit a store can build into repeat purchases, because the shopper is buying an experience as much as a product.

That repeat behavior is easy to underestimate until you look for it: a shopper who designed one gift last quarter is a strong candidate to design another one this quarter, and every return visit is a chance to sell into a different print side, method, or option than the one they picked last time.

### Setting it up

Turning this into higher average order value on your own store is mostly configuration, not development work:

- Turn on multi-side customization for the products where it makes sense, and define each side's print area in the [Design Lab](/design-lab).
- Price each side individually, along with any print methods or product options you offer, so the price a shopper sees always matches what they've actually designed.
- Set quantity discount ranges for the group sizes your customers actually order in, so bulk orders get a fair price without you manually quoting them.

None of these require a redesign of your store or your product catalog. They're pricing rules — set once on [pricing](/pricing), then applied by Custy on every order a shopper designs, at whatever complexity and quantity they choose.

Personalization raises average order value because it gives shoppers real decisions to make, and gives merchants a pricing engine that reflects every one of those decisions back at checkout. The stores that get the most out of it aren't the ones with the flashiest design tool — they're the ones that priced their sides, print methods, and quantities deliberately, so nothing a shopper designs goes unpriced.
