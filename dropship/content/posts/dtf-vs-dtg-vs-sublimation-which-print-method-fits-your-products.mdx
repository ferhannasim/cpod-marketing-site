Every new print-on-demand seller eventually asks the same question: DTF, DTG, sublimation, or embroidery? The honest answer is that there's no single best print method — there's only the method that fits the product in front of you. Get this decision right and your prints hold their color, survive the wash, and feel the way a customer expects them to feel. Get it wrong, and you'll ship goods that look fine on day one and disappoint by day thirty. Most new sellers pick a method by habit or hearsay rather than by matching it to the fabric and finish they're actually selling. This guide walks through what each method is good at, so you can choose deliberately instead of guessing.

## What DTF printing does well

Direct-to-film, or DTF, prints a design onto a special film, adds a powder adhesive, and heat-presses the whole thing onto the garment. Because the design lives on top of a transfer layer rather than soaking into the fibers, DTF doesn't care much what fabric it's landing on. Cotton, poly blends, and dark garments all take a DTF transfer the same way a light garment does, and colors stay vivid because a white underbase sits beneath the ink. That combination of fabric flexibility and color strength is why DTF has become the workhorse for general apparel — tees, hoodies, sweatshirts — where sellers need one process that works across an entire catalog rather than picking a new technique for every product. The finish has a slight hand-feel from the transfer layer, but it stretches with the fabric and holds up to regular washing far better than sellers expect from a transfer method. If you only learn one print process before launching a store, learn this one — see the full [DTF FAQ](/faq/dtf) for fabric compatibility and care detail.

## Where DTG shines

Direct-to-garment, or DTG, is closer to inkjet printing than transfer printing: the machine prints ink straight into the fibers of the shirt itself. There's no film, no adhesive layer, and no raised texture — just ink absorbed into cotton. That's what gives DTG its signature soft hand-feel; a customer can barely tell there's a print there at all until they look closely. DTG also handles photographic detail and gradient shading better than any transfer method, because it lays down continuous tone rather than a single pressed layer. The tradeoff is that DTG performs best, almost exclusively, on 100 percent cotton. Poly blends and dark garments don't take the ink the same way, so DTG is the right call specifically when the product is a cotton tee or tote and the design leans toward photo-realistic art, fine gradients, or subtle color blending rather than bold, flat graphics.

## When sublimation wins

Sublimation works differently again: heat turns the ink into a gas that bonds directly into polyester fibers or a poly-coated surface, so the color becomes part of the material rather than sitting on it. That means there's no texture, no cracking, and no peeling, because there's nothing sitting on the surface left to crack or peel. It's also the only method here that prints edge-to-edge without a visible border, which is why sublimation owns two categories almost outright: drinkware and all-over prints. Mugs, water bottles, and other hard, coated items only work with sublimation, and any design that needs to wrap fully around a garment or product with no white space at the edges belongs here too. The tradeoff is real: sublimation needs a polyester or coated substrate, so it isn't an option for a plain cotton tee. Read the [sublimation FAQ](/faq/sublimation) for substrate details, and review the [sublimation printing notice](/sublimation-printing-notice) before listing a sublimation product, since the expectations around fabric and finish differ from DTF.

## And embroidery?

Embroidery isn't a printing method at all — it's stitched thread, and that changes everything about where it belongs in a catalog. A stitched logo reads as premium in a way no printed design can, which is why embroidery is the standard for hats, polos, and anywhere a brand mark needs to look tailored rather than printed. What it can't do is reproduce a photo, a gradient, or fine detail; thread has a minimum stitch width, so embroidery is built for clean logos and simple wordmarks, not artwork. Reserve it for exactly that use case and lean on printing for everything else in the catalog.

## How to choose for your store

In practice, the decision follows the product, not personal preference:

- Cotton tees and hoodies with bold, colorful graphics: DTF.
- Cotton-only items where a soft feel and photographic detail matter more than fabric flexibility: DTG.
- Mugs, drinkware, or any all-over, edge-to-edge design: sublimation.
- Hats, polos, or a logo that needs to look stitched and premium: embroidery.
