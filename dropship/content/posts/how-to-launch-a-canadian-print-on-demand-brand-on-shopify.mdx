Most print-on-demand advice written for Shopify sellers assumes production happens in the United States, and everyone downstream — supplier, seller, customer — just works around it. If you're building a brand for Canadian customers, that default works against you. Cross-border shipments mean longer transit windows, customs handling that can stall a package for days, and duty questions your customer never expected to answer when they were just trying to buy a hoodie. None of that shows up in a demo. All of it shows up in your reviews.

The fix is straightforward: produce where you sell. A brand built on Canadian production, shipping to Canadian addresses, sidesteps the whole category of problems cross-border dropshipping creates. Delivery times become something you can state plainly and hit, and "will this arrive without a customs surprise" stops being a question customers have to ask. DropShipPOD is built around exactly that model — production happens in Canada, so what you promise is what your customer experiences on their doorstep.

Here's how to put that model to work, from picking what to sell through the day you flip your store live.

## Why domestic production is the whole foundation

Delivery speed is the most obvious benefit, but it's not the only one. A cross-border order has more places to go wrong: it can sit at a border facility, get flagged for extra paperwork, or arrive with an unexpected duty charge that lands on your customer instead of you — and your customer blames your store, not the supply chain behind it. Domestic production removes that failure category entirely. There's no border to cross, so there's no border surprise to manage, explain, or refund. Your delivery promise is a real promise, not a best-case estimate with an asterisk. A customer who gets exactly what was promised, on time, orders again and tells a friend; a customer who gets an unexpected charge or a delay usually doesn't.

## Picking a niche instead of a category

Before you touch the storefront, decide who the brand is actually for. "T-shirts" isn't a niche — it's a product type, and it competes against every other seller of t-shirts on price alone. A niche is an audience with a reason to buy that has nothing to do with price. The [catalog](/catalog) organizes this thinking into eight ready-to-use directions: athletic, college and Greek life; weddings and events; military; sports and teams; religious organizations; fundraising and charity; first responders; and holidays. Each has a built-in reason to buy — team pride, a wedding party, a unit reunion, a cause — that a generic tee never gets. Pick one, or a variation close to it, and every design decision that follows gets easier because you already know who you're designing for.

## Setting up the store: import, markup, publish

Once you know the niche, the mechanics are the same three steps for every product you add. First, import the blanks that fit your niche from the in-app catalog. Second, set your markup before anything goes live; retail price is base cost plus markup, and the app enforces that a markup exists before a product can publish, so nothing goes out priced at cost by mistake. Third, publish to your storefront — variants, images and options included in the same action. That sequence is designed to be repeated as fast as you can add new designs, not treated as a one-time setup task. The complete rundown is on the [how it works](/how-it-works) page.

## The pre-launch checklist

Before you announce the store, walk through four things a niche brand can't afford to get wrong.

Size charts first: a customer buying event or team apparel is often buying for other people — a whole wedding party, a whole roster — and a wrong fit multiplies across every person in the order. Point customers to the [size charts](/size-charts) before checkout, not after a return request.

Delivery expectations second: state actual transit windows, not vague "a few business days" language. Because production and shipping both happen domestically, the numbers you state are numbers you can hit — check specifics on the [delivery](/delivery) page and surface them before a customer buys, not buried in a policy page they never open.

Artwork approval third: niche merch lives and dies by whether the design looks right on the actual product, not just a screen. Keep your process for reviewing artwork and mockups before anything prints clear — the full standard is on the [artwork approval](/artwork-approval) page.

Production location fourth — this one is about how you talk about the brand, not just how it operates. Say plainly that goods are produced in Canada. For a domestic customer weighing a niche seller against a big anonymous marketplace, that's a real, checkable reason to trust a small store with an order that matters to them.

## Launching with confidence

A Canadian print-on-demand brand doesn't need a bigger catalog or a louder ad budget to compete — it needs a promise it can keep. Domestic production makes the delivery promise real, a focused niche gives customers a reason to choose you over a generic search result, and a clean import-markup-publish workflow means you can act on new ideas the same day you have them. Get those three right before you launch, and the checklist above becomes routine maintenance instead of a source of returns and complaints.
