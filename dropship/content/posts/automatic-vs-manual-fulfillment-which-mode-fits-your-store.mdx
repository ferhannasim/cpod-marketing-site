Every print-on-demand seller eventually faces the same tradeoff: how much of the order process do you want to happen without you, and how much do you want to see before it becomes real? Speed and control pull in opposite directions, and most platforms force you to pick one permanently, before you've even shipped your first order. DropShipPOD doesn't ask you to choose once and live with it — it gives you both modes, lets you set them per store, and lets you change your mind as your business changes. The real question isn't which mode is better; it's which mode fits the store you're running right now.

## What each mode actually does

Automatic fulfillment is the hands-off path. The moment a customer's payment lands, your saved card is charged the base cost and the order is forwarded to production — no click, no review screen, no delay between the sale and the print floor. Once it's switched on, a working day of sales can go from storefront to production without you touching a single order.

Manual review mode inserts a deliberate checkpoint. Orders are held for your review the moment they come in, and nothing is charged or produced until you press Place order yourself. Your saved card isn't touched until that moment — the charge happens on your action, not on the customer's payment. It's slower by design: a human decision sits between "a customer bought this" and "this is now in production."

Neither mode is a lesser version of the other. They're built for different situations, and the right choice depends on what's happening in your catalog on a given day.

## When automatic fulfillment wins

Automatic mode earns its keep once a product has already proven itself. If a design has weeks of order history with no surprises — no sizing complaints, no artwork issues, no returns worth worrying about — there's very little a manual review step would catch that a customer's payment hasn't already validated. At that point, the review step isn't protecting you from risk; it's just adding latency between a sale and a shipment.

Volume changes the calculation too. A store running dozens of orders a day on an established catalog can't realistically hand-review every one without turning order management into a full-time job. Automatic mode removes that bottleneck, letting the store scale sales without scaling the overhead. For sellers who genuinely want a hands-off operation — set it up once, check in periodically, let the orders run — automatic mode is the closest match to that goal. ## When manual review wins

Manual mode earns its keep in exactly the situations automatic mode is built to skip past. A brand-new design hasn't accumulated any order history yet, so there's no proof it looks right in practice rather than on a screen. A quick review before the first several orders ship catches a mismatched size range, an artwork placement issue, or a color that reads differently in production than it did in a mockup — problems that are cheap to fix before a customer receives them and expensive to fix after.

Manual mode also fits any store that needs a custom check on top of the standard workflow: a personalization step to confirm, a name or number to double-check, a customer note that needs eyes on it first. Automatic mode has no place for that judgment call; manual mode is built around it.

There's a financial reason to choose manual mode too, separate from quality control. Because your card is only charged when you press Place order rather than the instant a customer pays, manual review gives you a buffer to pace your own cash flow — batching orders, timing charges, or reviewing the day's sales before committing to production costs. For a newer store still finding its footing, that pacing can matter as much as the quality check itself.

## Switching between modes

Because the choice isn't permanent, it doesn't have to be made once and defended forever. The fulfillment mode is a setting inside the app, meant to be revisited as your catalog and your confidence in it change. A common pattern is starting new products in manual review — watching the first batch of orders closely — then switching that product to automatic once it has a track record of clean orders. Established lines can stay on automatic indefinitely, while the next batch of new designs goes back through the same manual check.

That flexibility is the point of offering both modes at all. A store isn't one thing forever — it has proven products and untested ones, calm weeks and volume weeks, at the same time. Automatic and manual fulfillment aren't competing philosophies to pick between; they're two settings for two different moments in a product's life, and the fastest-growing stores tend to use both, on different products, in the same week. Full detail on when your card is charged under each mode is on the [pricing](/pricing) page.

Speed and control aren't actually opposites once you stop treating fulfillment mode as a single, permanent decision. Match the mode to the product in front of you, and you get the hands-off scale of automatic fulfillment on what's already working, and the checkpoint of manual review on what still needs to prove itself.
